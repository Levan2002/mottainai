Best before vs use by: what the dates really mean

The short answer: a "use by" date is about safety, and a "best before" date is about quality. Knowing the difference stops you from throwing away good food, and from eating something you shouldn't. Here's how to read the dates on a pack.

Use by: a safety date

A use by date appears on food that can become unsafe quickly: fresh meat and fish, ready meals, prepared salads and many chilled foods. Don't eat these after the date, even if they look and smell fine, because the bacteria that make you ill don't always change how food looks or smells.

Follow the storage instructions on the pack too. "Keep refrigerated" and "use within two days of opening" are part of the date. If you want to keep something longer, you can freeze it up to the use by date, and follow the instructions on the pack once it has thawed. The UK government explains this in its guide to best before and use-by dates.

Best before: a quality date

Best before tells you when the maker expects the food to be at its best: flavour, texture, colour and crunch. After the date it isn't automatically unsafe. Dried pasta, rice, tinned food, biscuits, crisps and frozen foods are usually fine well past it if they've been stored properly, though they may slowly lose quality. Look at the food, smell it, and taste a little. If it looks or smells wrong, throw it away.

What about "sell by" and "display until"?

These dates are for the shop, not for you. They help staff rotate stock. You can normally eat the food after the sell by date, as long as you stored it properly and it's still within its use by or best before date.

What does EXP mean?

"EXP" or "expires" is ambiguous on food. On medicines, it means don't use after. On food it often means the same as best before, but not always, so for perishable foods such as meat, fish and dairy, treat it like a use by date. When in doubt, look for another date or follow the storage instructions.

Dates in other countries

In the United States, apart from infant formula, federal law doesn't require food to carry a date, and the dates you see are usually the manufacturer's estimate of quality, not a safety date. The US Department of Agriculture explains this on its food product dating page. Elsewhere, the two kinds of date have separate names. In Germany, Mindesthaltbarkeitsdatum (MHD) is best before and Verbrauchsdatum is use by. In Japan, 賞味期限 is the best-before date, and 消費期限 is the use-by date for foods that spoil quickly. The Japanese word mottainai is the regret of letting something good go to waste.

How to decide what to do

  • Use by, and the date has passed: throw it away. Don't taste-test it.
  • Use by, and it's a day or two away: cook and eat it, or freeze it today.
  • Best before, past the date: check how it was stored, then look, smell and taste. Dry and tinned food is often fine.
  • Opened: check the pack for an "after opening" instruction. It overrides the printed date.
  • Baby formula, and people at higher risk: never use formula after its date. If you're pregnant, elderly or have a weakened immune system, be stricter with chilled foods.

None of this replaces the advice of your local food safety authority. When you're unsure, check the pack and err on the side of caution.
